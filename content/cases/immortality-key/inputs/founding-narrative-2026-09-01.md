In September 2020, a lawyer with degrees in classics published a book arguing that Western civilization's central rite began as a drug. Brian Muraresku's *The Immortality Key* spent weeks on the bestseller lists, reached millions of podcast listeners, and revived a hypothesis that mainstream classics had spent forty years declining: that the kykeon — the barley drink at the climax of the Eleusinian Mysteries, the initiation that Sophocles, Pindar, and Cicero describe as the most profound experience available to an ancient Greek — was a psychedelic, and that the earliest Greek-speaking Christians inherited a version of it as the original Eucharist. A "religion with no name," running underneath the official ones, with a spiked cup as its sacrament.

The thesis sounds like one claim. It is at least thirteen, stacked in a ladder — and the honest state of the evidence changes drastically from rung to rung. The bottom of the ladder has been settled by laboratory chemistry within the last six years. The top is contradicted by the earliest documents we have. And the rungs in between — the ones the book actually stands on — turn on a small number of physical tests that, remarkably, nobody has ever run.

## What the chemistry has settled

Start with what is no longer worth arguing about. For most of the twentieth century, claims about ancient drug use ran on iconography and inference — a mushroom-shaped carving here, a suggestive poppy goddess there — and could be dismissed accordingly. That era is over, ended by residue chemistry. On the altars of an eighth-century BCE Judahite shrine at Tel Arad, cannabis burned with animal dung. In human hair from a Bronze Age cult cave on Menorca, measured directly in the tissue: ephedrine, atropine, scopolamine. In a Ptolemaic Egyptian mug shaped like the god Bes: a fermented multi-plant preparation including harmal and blue lotus. At a California rock-art site, chewed wads of hallucinogenic Datura tucked into the ceiling cracks. [People in the ancient world really did take mind-altering substances in religious settings]{claim=TIK-C001} — that is now direct chemical fact, several times over, from independent teams on independent materials.

{plate:IMG-TIK-P01}

The first-century pharmacopeia points the same way for wine in particular. Dioscorides describes [wines deliberately compounded with psychoactive plants]{claim=TIK-C002} — henbane, mandrake, black nightshade, this last credited with producing "not unpleasant visions." Greek routinely called wine a *pharmakon*, a drug. Ancient wine was a solvent and a delivery vehicle, not just a beverage.

So the floor of the ladder holds. The trouble — and the reason this case exists — is that the floor is routinely presented as if it supported the penthouse. A cannabis altar in Iron Age Judah is excellent evidence that ancient ritual pharmacology existed. It is no evidence at all that ergot was in the cup at Eleusis, and less than none that a Christian in Corinth drank it. Every rung must pay its own way.

## The drink with a published recipe

What was actually in the kykeon? Antiquity left one recipe. In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter — the founding text of the Mysteries — the grieving goddess refuses wine and asks instead for [barley meal and water mixed with soft pennyroyal mint]{claim=TIK-C003}. That is the whole list. No ancient source names ergot; none describes any processing step beyond mixing; the drink reads as a rustic barley posset with an aromatic herb.

{plate:IMG-TIK-P02}

The hypothesis's founders knew this, and built on it anyway — because of what barley implies. Ergot, the fungus *Claviceps purpurea*, grows on cereal grains, and its alkaloids are chemical cousins of LSD, which Albert Hofmann first synthesized from ergot derivatives. In 1978 Hofmann himself, with the mushroom pioneer R. Gordon Wasson and the classicist Carl Ruck, proposed in *The Road to Eleusis* that ergot on the Rarian plain's barley supplied the kykeon's secret. Classics mostly declined the idea, and it hibernated for decades in the entheogen literature — until Muraresku's book brought it back with two genuinely new kinds of support.

## The two best cards in the Greek hand

The first is archaeological, and it is the case's one hard physical datum. At Mas Castellar de Pontós in Catalonia — near the ancient Greek colony of Emporion, at a site with a sanctuary linked to Demeter and Persephone, the two goddesses of Eleusis — a 2002 analysis reported [fragments of ergot inside a ceremonial vessel and in the dental calculus of a young man buried there]{claim=TIK-C004}. Substance, ingestion, and cult context, together, in a Greek-influenced Iberian setting. It is the closest thing the hypothesis has to a smoking gun, and its limitations are exactly as large as they sound: one analysis, performed twenty-four years ago, never independently replicated, at a site a thousand kilometers from Eleusis — and known to the modern debate mostly through summaries that have a documented tendency to harden "fragments of ergot" into "hallucinogenic alkaloids chemically detected."

{plate:IMG-TIK-P04}

The second is chemical, and it arrived in February 2026. The standing objection to the ergot hypothesis was always toxicity: raw ergot causes ergotism — gangrene, convulsions — not transcendence. A team including Ruck and other veterans of the hypothesis put the counter-proposal to the test at last: they refluxed powdered ergot in wood-ash lye, the strong alkali ancient Greeks could and did make, and showed that [the toxic ergopeptides hydrolyze completely while measurable amounts of the psychoactive lysergamides LSA and iso-LSA appear]{claim=TIK-C005}. About a gram of ergot per initiate, they extrapolate, could have supplied an active dose. "Ergot would have poisoned everybody" is no longer available as an objection at the level of possibility.

Read the fine print, though, and the experiment cuts both ways. Full detoxification happened only at the strongest alkalinity, with two hours of controlled hot processing; weaker or shorter treatments left poison detectable. The feasible pathway is therefore a *technology* — a specific, careful, multi-hour alkaline operation — and no ancient source attests anything of the kind at Eleusis. The authors are candid that they demonstrated feasibility, not history. And the result has not yet been independently replicated, which matters more than usual here, because the team includes the hypothesis's originators and members of an entheogen-research center: sympathetic hands, doing real chemistry, that a disinterested laboratory should now repeat.

There is also a pharmacological gap the popular telling skips. "Related to LSD" carries the story, but LSA is substantially weaker than LSD, and the human literature on it — dominated by morning-glory-seed exposures — is a record of [hallucinations and euphoria mixed with sedation, nausea, autonomic effects, and anxiety]{claim=TIK-C006}. Whether the reconstructed preparation, at achievable doses, reliably delivers the best night of your life rather than an unpleasant sedated one is an open question no one has tested, because no one has ever profiled the full reconstructed extract.

## The empty cell

{plate:IMG-TIK-P05}

Here is the strangest fact in the whole controversy, and both sides agree on it: **no vessel from Eleusis has ever been chemically analyzed.** Not one. The 2026 paper says so while calling for exactly that analysis; the sanctuary's leading epigrapher says so while dismissing the book. Two millennia of cult, an excavated sanctuary, museum storerooms of ceramics — and the decisive measurement has never been made, because systematic residue research at the site has never been conducted or permitted. Proponents read the blank as "not yet tested." Skeptics read it as "nothing found." It is an untested absence, and it makes [the headline Greek claim — deliberately prepared ergot in the Eleusinian kykeon]{claim=TIK-C007} — a hypothesis with real circumstantial scaffolding and an empty cell where its direct evidence should be.

The scaffolding should not be oversold, and the counter-record is specific. The canonical recipe names nothing psychoactive. The sanctuary's finances were published on stone and publicly audited — Kevin Clinton, who has spent a career on the Eleusinian inscriptions, notes that the accounts track the sacred grain into sacrifices and dedications, with no expenditure that looks like drug production. And the alternative explanation is not "nothing happened." [The Mysteries reliably produced overwhelming experiences; the dispute is over the generator]{claim=TIK-C008} — and nine days of fasting, procession, secrecy, sensory deprivation and release, ritual drama, and mass emotion in a darkened hall is the mainstream candidate, a machine that needs no secret ingredient, no supply chain, and no dosing control, and that demonstrably ran stable for nearly two thousand years. Modern psychedelic trials prove the pharmacological route to mystical experience is real; nothing yet proves Eleusis used it.

## The Christian half, where the ladder thins

The book's second act needs more than Eleusis: it needs the practice to cross into Christianity. The crossing is argued through Dionysus. [The Gospel of John engages Dionysian imagery]{claim=TIK-C009} — water into wine at Cana, "I am the true vine" — and some serious scholars do read those passages as deliberately painting Jesus in the wine god's colors. But a literary relationship is a claim about texts and their symbolic environment. To get pharmacology across, you need the Dionysian wine to have been psychedelic (Muraresku himself, at Harvard, called that "a big if"), and then you need an actual community to have transferred the practice rather than the imagery.

What does the early Christian record show? The earliest descriptions of the Eucharist we possess — Paul's in 1 Corinthians, the Didache's table prayers, Justin Martyr's detailed account around 155 CE — describe bread and a cup of wine (Justin: wine mixed with water) in Jewish thanksgiving form, descended from Second-Temple meal liturgy. Justin even addresses the pagan parallels directly, and his framing runs the wrong way for the inheritance thesis: the Mithraic meal, he says, is the devils' *imitation* of the Christian one. The nearest thing to a drugged cup anywhere in the early record is a hostile one: Irenaeus's polemic against the Gnostic Marcus, whom he accuses of a conjuring trick with cup colors and of compounding love-philters — fraud and seduction, not sacramental psychedelia. On the current documentary record, [the claim that the original Eucharist was a psychedelic potion]{claim=TIK-C011} is contradicted; [the weaker claim that some early community, somewhere, spiked its cup]{claim=TIK-C010} is neither supported nor refuted, because — the pattern by now is familiar — **no securely identified early-Christian liturgical vessel has ever been chemically tested either.**

{plate:IMG-TIK-P06}

The experts the book itself leans on are blunt about the gap. Patrick McGovern, the Penn Museum's pioneer of biomolecular archaeology and an adviser Muraresku consulted, calls the scientific evidence connecting psychedelics to early Christianity "extremely weak to nonexistent." Jan Bremmer, the mystery-cult specialist: "Christianity was not a mystery cult." Bart Ehrman: not plausible. Even the sympathetic reviews cut the same way — Jerry Brown, himself a psychedelic-Christianity author, praises the Eleusis material and concludes that the book "fails to make a compelling case for its grand theory," repeatedly presenting speculation as fact. And Muraresku, in his scholarly register, concedes more than his marketing does: he does not defend a psychedelic Last Supper ("I don't hang my hat on that claim"), grants the Dionysian premise is unproven, and asks the transmission question himself — "Where are the drugs?"

That transmission rung — [a psychoactive sacrament actually passing from the Greek mysteries into the Christian cup]{claim=TIK-C012} — is the weakest load-bearing element in the structure. Cultural contact is real; wine-and-immortality symbolism is shared; and none of that is surprising without transmission, because wine and immortality are among the most common religious symbols in the ancient Mediterranean. Beneath it sits a scholarly framework — [the Eucharist as an outgrowth of pagan mystery ritual rather than Jewish meal practice]{claim=TIK-C013} — that early-twentieth-century scholarship entertained and the modern field has rejected. If the framework is wrong, any future evidence for a psychedelic Christian cup would have to establish its own transmission vector rather than inherit one.

## The money, and a disclosure

The hypothesis now has institutional machinery. The Yale Ancient Pharmacology Program — conceived with Muraresku's involvement and supported by the Athanatos Foundation he founded — is building exactly the residue-analysis capacity this case's decisive tests require, and its published funding statement names its supporters: E. Jhong, the Robert and MeiLi Hefner Foundation, the Athanatos Foundation, and B. Muraresku.

E. Jhong is this project's founder. Readers should know that when weighing this case, and the case records it on every source it touches: the research program best positioned to test the Eleusinian and early-Christian claims is funded by the hypothesis's author, his foundation, and the founder of the site you are reading. Funding is provenance, not refutation — chemistry stands or falls on replication, not on who paid for the instrument — but independence accounting is half of this case's method, and the accounting must include ourselves. Any future YAPP result entering this ledger will carry that flag, and the same discipline applies in reverse: critics' motives are context, never evidence about what was in an ancient cup.

## Where this case stands

Rung by rung: ancient ritual pharmacology, established. Feasible ergot chemistry, demonstrated once, by interested hands, awaiting independent replication. Ergot at Eleusis, weakly supported — one unreplicated Iberian analogue, a workable mechanism, an empty cell at the sanctuary itself. The drug-versus-ritual generator question, unresolved and honestly so. A psychedelic original Eucharist, contradicted by every early document we have. A spiked cup somewhere in early Christianity, untested. Transmission, the grand thesis, running on symbolism and possibility.

What makes this case unusual on this site is how cheap the decisive evidence would be. The vessels exist. The methods are routine — the same panels that found cannabis at Arad and alkaloids in Menorcan hair. Residue analysis of curated Eleusinian ceramics, re-analysis of the Mas Castellar samples, a preregistered survey of early-Christian cups: any one of these would move the case more than everything published since 1978 combined.
