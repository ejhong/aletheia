In July 2019, at a conference in Paris, a gravitational-wave physicist opened a box in public. Hartmut Grote builds instruments for a living — the kind that measure distortions in spacetime smaller than a proton — and for a decade he had run, on the side, careful experiments on whether human intention can influence a random physical process. This one was armored against every known way of fooling yourself: two hundred participants, and a "blind analysis" protocol in which the entire evaluation software was written, tested, and locked before anyone looked at the data. The unblinding — the moment the analysis runs and the answer appears — happened live, on stage. [The main result was null, falling well within what chance predicts]{claim=MPI-C002}.

For most theories about anomalous mind–matter effects, that is where the story ends. For the theory Grote was testing, it is where the story gets strange. The Model of Pragmatic Information — MPI, developed by the German physicist and parapsychology researcher Walter von Lucadou since the 1980s — does not merely tolerate failed replications. [It predicts them, in print, as a consequence of its central axiom]{claim=MPI-C005}. On MPI's account, the box Grote opened was always going to be empty, or nearly so, *because he had built it so well*.

A theory that predicts its own replication failures sounds like a joke about unfalsifiability, and one of this case's jobs is to take that worry with full seriousness. But MPI is more interesting than the joke version. It made its predictions before the failures happened. It proposed its own experiment, with a positive signature that was supposed to show up — and for a while, in its inventors' hands, it did. And the question it was built to answer is real: after a century of laboratory parapsychology, effects keep appearing in initial studies and dissolving in stricter ones, a pattern documented by insiders as much as critics. Something produces that pattern. The candidates are a law of nature or the ordinary statistics of nothing, and the two are harder to tell apart than they should be.

## The theory, without the mathematics

MPI starts by refusing the assumption that psi effects, if real, are signals — something sent from a mind to a machine, however weakly. Instead it treats them as *correlations*: coordinated behavior between a person and a physical system that belong, temporarily, to one closed system of meaning, the way two entangled particles belong to one quantum state. In quantum mechanics, entangled particles show correlations that no signal explains — and, famously, those correlations cannot be used to transmit a message. [A generalization of this structure — keeping complementarity and entanglement, dropping the physical constants — was formalized as "weak" or Generalized Quantum Theory in 2002]{claim=MPI-C004}, and MPI sits inside it as the special case where the entangled parts are a person and an apparatus, bound by what the theory calls pragmatic information: information that matters to the system receiving it.

From this one commitment everything distinctive follows. If the correlations cannot carry a message, then no experiment can be allowed to turn them into one. A perfectly replicable psi experiment would be a telegraph — press here, read the answer there — and the model's "no-transmission" axiom forbids telegraphs. So [when a protocol becomes reliable enough to exploit, the correlation must fade, reverse sign, or move somewhere else]{claim=MPI-C005}: to a different variable, a different part of the system, a different analysis. Parapsychologists had names for these embarrassments long before MPI — the decline effect, the displacement effect, [catalogued across the field's own literature as its most reliable feature]{claim=MPI-C003}. MPI's wager was to promote the embarrassments to theorems.

## The experiment the theory built for itself

A theory that only explained failure would explain too much. Lucadou understood this, and in the 1980s designed an experiment his model says *should* work — the correlation-matrix experiment, and the reason this case is testable at all.

A participant sits before a display driven by a physical random event generator and presses keys, trying to "influence" it.

{plate:IMG-MPI-P01} Nothing about the session is scored for success; there is no hit rate. Instead, the experimenters record many psychological variables (the pattern of key presses) and many physical variables (the machine's behavior), and afterward compute every correlation between the two sets — a matrix of hundreds or thousands of cells. [Because no single cell is "the result," no signal can be coded, the no-transmission axiom is respected — and the entanglement correlations are permitted to appear as a surplus of statistically significant cells, scattered unpredictably through the matrix]{claim=MPI-C006}. The design's own inventors conceded in advance that this would "never... convince every sceptic." It is psi research designed to be shy.

And in its inventors' hands, it worked. [Lucadou's experiments from 1986 through 2006 reported roughly twice as many significant correlations in experimental sessions as in control runs]{claim=MPI-C001}. In 2020, a larger two-lab replication led by the health researcher Harald Walach — rebuilt hardware, 2,025-cell matrix, evaluation against ten thousand simulated experiments — [reported a significant surplus again]{claim=MPI-C007}, in an American Psychological Association journal.

## The record, under pressure

Then the protocols tightened, and the pattern every parapsychologist knows reasserted itself.

Walach's team convened an international consortium, froze a consensus protocol, deposited it publicly before data collection, and ran two further replications with the same equipment. Both were null. To their credit, they published the failures under a title of rare candor — "Nailing Jelly: The Replication Problem Seems to Be Unsurmountable" — and conceded that [the matrix experiment "in and of itself is not a replicable paradigm"]{claim=MPI-C002}. In the same paper they described their own 2020 success as "significant but fragile," its significance breaking down under improved methods of analysis.

Grote's independent series traced the same arc in miniature: a significant result in 2015, then in 2017 a study where [all three analyses he had defined in advance came out null — while a variant he constructed after seeing the data came out significant]{claim=MPI-C008}, and finally the 2019 blind analysis, published in 2021: null. At Edinburgh's Koestler Parapsychology Unit, Ana Flores ran five replications for her doctoral thesis, some with Lucadou's own equipment, and got a genuinely mixed answer: three of five significant under analyses designed to be more conservative than the originals. Her later online version, with 726 participants, split down the middle in a theoretically suggestive way — significant with a true random source, null with a deterministic pseudo-random one — though that result remains an unreviewed preprint. And the model's one field test — a three-year, sixteen-country study of spontaneous poltergeist-type cases, testing MPI's prediction that documenting a case in detail suppresses it — collapsed to fourteen usable cases and decided nothing.

Read as a whole, the record is exactly what MPI said it would be: early surpluses, fading and scattering as protocols hardened. The trouble is that it is also exactly what nothing-at-all would look like.

## The steelman: what noise does

The conventional explanation deserves to be stated at full strength, because in this case it is unusually well armed — nearly every mechanism it needs has been caught operating somewhere in this very literature.

Start with the statistics. Counting "significant cells" in a matrix only means something if the cells are independent, and they are not: the psychological variables correlate strongly with one another, so significant cells arrive in bundles, and the founding analyses counted the bundles as if each cell were separate testimony. [Simulations respecting that dependence shrink the original headline results]{claim=MPI-C009} — a critique reported not by hostile skeptics but from inside the research program itself. Add the documented failure modes: a celebrated correlational micro-PK effect in a neighboring research line turned out to be a software coding error, found and honorably published by its own authors; Grote's 2017 post-hoc rescue shows how flexible analysis conjures significance from null data; and the broader random-generator literature that the matrix program grew from was assessed by the largest meta-analysis in the field's flagship journal as most economically explained by publication bias. [On this reading the whole arc — surplus, decline, displacement — is regression to the mean acting on selected noise, and MPI is a quantum-flavored description of the null hypothesis]{claim=MPI-C008}.

The steelman is not airtight. A simulation study by researchers sympathetic to the field found that questionable research practices at realistic frequencies explained about sixty percent of the flagship ganzfeld effect — a long way, but not all the way. Walach's 2020 replication was preregistered and adequately powered, and reported its surplus anyway. Flores's three-of-five survived her own conservative analyses. The mundane account predicts that stricter methods kill the effect; mostly they did, but not cleanly, not everywhere.

## The crux

So the case comes down to a question that is only partly about data: [is the decline-and-displacement record evidence of a real, lawfully shy correlation]{claim=MPI-C007} — or is MPI so constructed that no record could count against it? When the strictest test comes back positive, the model is confirmed; when it comes back null, the model predicted that too. The proponents' own response to their failed replications — that failure "is likely part of the systematic nature of such effects" — is, depending on where you stand, a theory being consistent or [a theory being unfalsifiable in practice]{claim=MPI-C010}.

The honest answer is that the insulation is a habit, not a theorem. MPI does expose itself: its axiom forbids any stable, exploitable psi channel anywhere, ever — a risky universal claim that one replicable experiment would demolish. It nominated the matrix design as a positive prediction that could have failed from the start. And it asserts distinctive fine structure that no one has ever pinned down in advance: decline should be *faster when the initial effect was stronger*; displaced effects should reappear in *related* observables, not random ones. Those are testable claims. They have simply never been tested as predictions — only invoked, afterward, as explanations.

## What would settle it

That is the research agenda, and it is unusually concrete for a dispute this old. Freeze the fine structure: an adversarial replication, blind-analysis by construction, with the decline and displacement predictions specified quantitatively in advance and endorsed by the model's proponents as binding. Re-run the founding analyses under dependence-aware statistics — desk work that would either restore the program's origin story or dissolve it. Run the true-random versus pseudo-random comparison at equal power, one of the few places where MPI and the null hypothesis disagree about the future rather than the past. And ask the proponents, formally and on the record, what result they would accept as a refutation.

A theory that predicts its own elusiveness has one obligation above all others: to say what it forbids. Forty years in, that remains the one experiment the Model of Pragmatic Information has never quite run.
