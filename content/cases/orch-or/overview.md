For most of thirty years, Orch OR was the theory you invoked to end a conversation. Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff proposed in the mid-1990s that [each conscious moment is a gravitationally-induced collapse of a quantum state orchestrated across tubulin proteins inside neuronal microtubules]{claim=ORCH-C001}, and the field's reply was a calculation: the brain is warm, wet, and electrically noisy, quantum coherence there would last something like 10⁻¹³ seconds, and a neuron takes ten orders of magnitude longer to do anything. Case closed, and closed by a physicist rather than a partisan.

Between 2023 and 2026 the biological half stopped being purely speculative. Microtubules turned out to move electronic excitation further than standard theory predicts. Anesthetics measurably shorten that transport. A microtubule-stabilizing drug delays anesthetic unconsciousness in rats, then in mice. None of those results measured a coherence time, an entangled state, or a collapse event. Meanwhile an experiment under an Italian mountain killed the most natural version of Penrose's physics, an independent laboratory found the anesthesia effect running backwards, and AI systems began solving the kind of mathematics that motivated the whole idea in the first place.

The interesting thing about this case is how cleanly its rungs come apart — and how consistently the public argument, in both directions, refuses to let them.

## Five different propositions

Almost every confused exchange about Orch OR consists of silently sliding between these: quantum effects in microtubules, quantum coherence in microtubules, entanglement in microtubules, quantum computation in neurons, and Penrose gravitational collapse. The first is trivially true — all chemistry is quantum mechanics. The last is an unconfirmed proposal in the foundations of physics. They are not the same claim and evidence for one is not evidence for the next.

So the ladder below this article separates them, and the separation produces some verdicts that look strange until you see why. [Microtubules have collective electronic dynamics that a model of them as passive scaffolding does not predict]{claim=ORCH-C013} is assessed as well supported. [Neuronal microtubules sustain coherent states long enough and wide enough to support the computation the theory requires]{claim=ORCH-C010} is assessed as unresolved — not contradicted, *unmeasured*. And the headline thesis, which needs both, sits above them as unresolved on the strength of the weakest rung it depends on.

## What the microtubules actually do

{plate:IMG-ORCHOR-P01}

The pivotal experiment came out of Gregory Scholes's ultrafast spectroscopy laboratory at Princeton in 2023. Shine ultraviolet light on polymerized microtubules, watch how tryptophan fluorescence lifetimes change as you add a quencher, and infer how far electronic excitation travels before it dissipates. The answer: [about 6.6 nanometres, which the authors report is not sufficiently explained by accepted Förster resonance energy transfer models even after accounting for tryptophan–tyrosine interactions]{claim=ORCH-C020}. Their own summary is that microtubules are, unexpectedly, effective light harvesters. Polymerization state mattered; protofilament architecture did not — the kind of structure-dependence that argues against an artifact.

A partly independent group at Howard University then reported that [organized tryptophan networks in microtubules form collective superradiant states, with fluorescence quantum yield rising with hierarchical organization as their model predicts]{claim=ORCH-C021}. Microtubules are not inert beams. That is a real result and it would be worth knowing regardless of anyone's views about consciousness.

Now the part that proponent summaries tend to skip. 6.6 nanometres is roughly the length of a single tubulin dimer, and the physics is femtoseconds to picoseconds. Orch OR needs milliseconds, across many tubulins, ideally across many neurons. That is a shortfall of many orders of magnitude in both time and space, and none of it has been closed. The superradiance paper's arresting figure — collective states with radiative lifetimes of "tens of seconds" — is a measure of how weakly a state couples to the light field, not of how long it survives environmental dephasing. A state can be radiatively dark for seconds and decohere in femtoseconds.

The two numbers that would settle this rung do not exist. What exists instead is two calculations. Max Tegmark's 2000 paper put microtubule decoherence at [10⁻¹³ to 10⁻²⁰ seconds against neural dynamical timescales of 10⁻³ to 10⁻¹]{claim=ORCH-C010}, and Hagan, Hameroff and Tuszyński replied in the same journal two years later that Tegmark had modeled the wrong state — a soliton in superposition along the microtubule rather than the superposed protein conformations the theory proposes — and that correcting the assumptions yields 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ seconds. Both are right that the other is arguing about a model. Neither measured a living microtubule. And it is worth noticing that even the proponents' own revised figure falls one to two orders of magnitude short of the ~25 millisecond conscious moment their theory needs.

There is also a distinctive result from Anirban Bandyopadhyay's group at NIMS in Japan: [self-similar "triplet of triplet" resonance patterns in tubulin, in microtubules, and in a neuron's axon initial segment]{claim=ORCH-C022}, offered as the scale-bridging mechanism the theory requires to get from molecules to brains. It has stood unreplicated for over a decade. Eugene's own primer, which is otherwise sympathetic, calls independent replication of this work "the missing piece," and draws the comparison that matters: the photosynthetic coherence results of 2007 were replicated many times over and founded a field.

## The anesthesia experiments, in both directions

This is the tractable part of the case, and the part with an actual empirical fight in it.

{plate:IMG-ORCHOR-P02}

The proponent line runs like this. Anesthetics reduce microtubule exciton diffusion in the Princeton experiment. An anesthesia laboratory at Penn with no Orch OR commitment [identified β-tubulin as the photolabeled target of an anthracene anesthetic and found microtubule stabilizers raising the concentration needed to immobilize tadpoles]{claim=ORCH-C011}. And then the behavioral result: Michael Wiest's group at Wellesley gave rats a low dose of the microtubule stabilizer epothilone B and found they [took 69 seconds longer to lose the righting reflex under isoflurane, a large standardized effect, blinded, with a control for anesthesia tolerance]{claim=ORCH-C023}. They repeated the direction in mice.

Then there is the result that a fair map cannot omit. A group at Massachusetts General Hospital, independent of the Wellesley program, ran the fuller version of the experiment — randomized, blinded, four drugs, full concentration–response curves rather than latency at one concentration — and found [chronic epothilone D making mice markedly *more* sensitive to isoflurane, not less]{claim=ORCH-C024}. Worse for the simple story: the destabilizer vinblastine moved sensitivity the same direction as the stabilizer. No account based on microtubule stability predicts that. It suggests the operative variable might be general cellular disruption.

This does not refute the acute epothilone-B finding — different compound, different regimen, acute versus two weeks of dosing. It does refute the generalization that stabilizing microtubules protects consciousness from anesthetics, which is how the claim is usually presented in public. Credit where it is due: the Wellesley group cited the contradictory study in their own abstract rather than omitting it.

And the sharpest objection to the whole anesthesia arm comes from inside its flagship experiment. The Princeton paper also tested picrotoxin — a convulsant, pharmacologically the opposite of an anesthetic — and found it affecting the same assay comparably. If a convulsant and two anesthetics all suppress the signal, the assay is measuring how perturbable microtubule photophysics is by small hydrophobic molecules, not anything specific to anesthesia. The experiment that would resolve this is not exotic: a potency series across eight or more agents with nonimmobilizers and matched non-anesthetic controls, with the predicted correlation preregistered. It has not been run.

One genuinely strange data point sits off to the side. [Xenon isotopes bearing nuclear spin are 40 to 50% weaker anesthetics than spin-zero isotopes, at identical calculated polarizability]{claim=ORCH-C025} — published in *Anesthesiology*, randomized across 80 mice. Nuclear spin is a quantum property with no classical polarizability explanation available. It is hard to fit into a pharmacology with no quantum degrees of freedom in it. It also says nothing whatsoever about microtubules: any spin-sensitive molecular target anywhere would produce it.

## The physics half, tested under a mountain

Penrose's contribution is separable from all of the biology, and it is testable without any. He proposed in 1996 that [superpositions of sufficiently different mass distributions collapse objectively, with a lifetime set by the gravitational self-energy of the difference]{claim=ORCH-C030} — τ ≈ ℏ/E_G. This is a proposal in quantum foundations that mainstream physicists have reason to test for their own reasons.

They tested a version of it. If gravity collapses wavefunctions in the standard stochastic way, charged particles should faintly radiate. A team including Lajos Diósi — co-originator of the model being tested — looked with an ultra-low-background germanium detector at the Gran Sasso underground laboratory and [found nothing, tightening the bound on the model's spatial cutoff by three orders of magnitude and ruling out, in their own words, "the natural parameter-free version of the Diósi–Penrose model"]{claim=ORCH-C031}. Methodologically this is the best experiment anywhere in this case, and it came out negative. The same authors then applied their bound to Orch OR and judged it "highly implausible" — with a conditional they wrote deliberately: *when based on the simplest version of gravity-related dynamical collapse*.

A philosopher of physics, Kelvin McQueen, pressed on exactly that conditional: Penrose's published criterion is a characteristic-lifetime relation, not a stochastic master equation, and it makes no radiation prediction. That is correct, and it is why this rung is assessed as unresolved rather than refuted. But it should be said plainly that escaping an experimental bound by being less specified than the model that was tested is not a good position. It converts a refuted precise theory into an untested vague one.

## The argument that started it, and the machines now answering it

Penrose did not arrive at microtubules from biology. He arrived from Gödel. His argument, in the weaker form he retreated to under criticism, is that [human mathematical understanding is not the execution of any knowably sound formal algorithm]{claim=ORCH-C040} — and if mathematical insight is non-computable, then something in the physics of the brain must be non-computable too, which is what sent him looking for new physics in the first place.

Critics now say this has been overtaken by events, and the events are real. In May 2026, [an internal OpenAI model disproved a 1946 Erdős conjecture in discrete geometry, and DeepMind systems reached Lean-verified silver and then officially-graded gold medal standard at the International Mathematical Olympiad]{claim=ORCH-C041}. This case verifies the Erdős result not through OpenAI's announcement but through Will Sawin's arXiv paper, which states the result, names the people who checked it, and sharpens the bound — an independent mathematician building on the construction. The IMO results are firmer still: proofs machine-checked in Lean, methodology peer-reviewed in *Nature*, and the 2025 gold graded by IMO coordinators under contest conditions.

Here is where this case insists on a distinction that both sides skip. **These results bear on the motivation for Orch OR, not on its mechanism.** Penrose's Gödel argument is why he went looking; it is not a premise of the claim that microtubules do the looking. Refute it completely and every microtubule and anesthesia claim above stands exactly where it was — the biology would simply lose its original reason for having been proposed. Vindicate it completely and you still have no reason to think microtubules are the implementer rather than something else. That is why both claims in this theme are assessed as low-diagnosticity for the headline thesis, and it is the single most useful thing the ladder does in this case.

Within the theme, the argument is live. The Penrose-side response is that performance is not understanding, and that the relevant boundary is between results *inside* a framework and the invention of the framework. That response gets unexpected support from inside the laboratory doing the work: a DeepMind researcher who co-authored AlphaProof argues in a 2026 ICML position paper that these systems have mastered induction, are rapidly conquering deduction, and [remain structurally incapable of the abductive "jump" required to formulate the premises they then reason from]{claim=ORCH-C041}. Against that, the honest counter-objection is that an argument no possible achievement can touch has bought its safety with its content. And this evidence line carries its own error history: an October 2025 round of AI Erdős claims collapsed when the solutions turned out to be already in the literature, and a Christmas 2025 claim was withdrawn within hours when a 1977 resolution by Erdős himself surfaced.

> **The case editor's own view, labeled as such.** Eugene's essay on the Erdős problem concedes the scoreboard against his own sympathies: of the concrete examples Penrose offered, the testable ones have mostly settled against him — most cleanly the pawn-fortress chess position the Penrose Institute released in 2017, which modern engines handle correctly — and the examples aging best are the least testable. His argument is that the claim was never that any *particular* insight is permanently non-computable, but that the open-ended capacity to keep generating new frameworks is: "the escalator, not the steps." Each formalized insight becoming computable in hindsight is then predicted by the view rather than damaging to it. Whether that is depth or unfalsifiability is left to the reader; the essay declines to decide it, which is why it is usable here.

## The conventional account, taken seriously

The alternative is not a shrug. On anesthesia specifically it has causal evidence of a kind Orch OR does not: [a single engineered residue change in the GABA_A receptor β3 subunit abolishes the immobilizing action of etomidate and propofol and profoundly reduces their hypnotic effect]{claim=ORCH-C003}. Change one amino acid, lose the drug. Nothing remotely comparable exists for tubulin, and any serious treatment of this topic has to reckon with the asymmetry.

The boundary of that result is also worth stating, because it is where proponents have a legitimate opening. The same mutation only slightly reduced the effects of the volatile anesthetics enflurane and halothane, and the authors' own conclusion was that volatile agents "act via a broader spectrum of molecular targets." The volatiles are the agents most Orch OR experiments use. The door is not closed; it is just that nobody has walked through it yet.

Where the conventional account is thinnest is explanation rather than mechanism — though on the inhaled agents even the mechanism is unfinished, since Eger and colleagues report that two decades of focused investigation identified no ligand- or voltage-gated channel, and no combination of them, sufficient to mediate immobility. It gives an increasingly detailed account of how consciousness is switched off and no principled account of why any of that activity is experienced, or why a conscious moment should have a characteristic duration. Orch OR at least attempts both. Attempting an explanation is not evidence for it.

## The crux

The disagreement does not live where the public argument puts it. Not in whether quantum mechanics operates inside proteins, and no longer in whether microtubules are electrically and optically interesting — measurements now say they are.

It lives in one unmeasured gap: between demonstrated collective electronic transport over roughly one tubulin dimer on femtosecond physics, and the long-lived, spatially extended coherent state the theory requires. Nobody has specified a density-matrix observable in physiological microtubules, measured its dephasing at 37 °C, and tested whether anesthetics suppress it in proportion to clinical potency. Until that number exists, "warm and wet kills it" and "warm quantum biology saves it" are two calculations arguing with each other.

Thirty years is a long time for a theory's central quantity to go unmeasured, and the theory bears some responsibility for that: it has never published the threshold — required coherence time, coherence length, participating degrees of freedom — that would let a measurement count as pass or fail. Each new photophysics result gets absorbed rather than scored. That is a defect in the theory's specification rather than evidence against its content, but it is the reason a cheap desk study asking what Orch OR actually commits to numerically sits high on the research agenda below, next to the expensive spectroscopy that would answer it.
