In the summer of 1542, a starving Dominican friar floated down a river no European had ever descended, keeping notes. Gaspar de Carvajal was the chaplain of Francisco de Orellana's accidental expedition — fifty-odd men in a makeshift brigantine who had set out to find cinnamon and ended up crossing South America by water. What he recorded was not wilderness. [His chronicle describes populous riverbank settlements running for leagues, storehouses of food, travelled roads leading inland, fine ceramics, and organized polities — alternating with stretches he reports as empty]{claim=AMZ-C001}.

For most of the twentieth century, educated opinion held that he was lying, or at best embellishing. The Amazon of the textbooks was a "counterfeit paradise": soils too poor, protein too scarce, floods too brutal for anything beyond small shifting villages. An eyewitness account of crowded riverbanks was an embarrassment to be explained away — a friar magnifying marvels for patrons back home. And later travelers, arriving a century and more afterward, found mostly what the textbooks predicted: forest, and silence.

This case is about what happened when the instruments caught up with the argument — and about the discipline required to say precisely how much of the friar's world they have found.

## The account, and its two texts

Before weighing Carvajal against archaeology, there is a problem inside the text itself. His chronicle survives in two main manuscript families, and they do not say the same things. [The most famous numbers — 50,000 warriors, 10,000, 8,000, villages four and five leagues long — appear in the Medina/Muñoz branch but not in Oviedo's version, which preserves the same crowded world with one figure of about 5,000 warriors]{claim=AMZ-C002}. Whatever else is true, the extreme figures are textually unstable in a way the qualitative picture is not. A serious reading of Carvajal was never "believe him" or "dismiss him"; it is: trust the kind of world he described, refuse his arithmetic.

And his description does not stand alone. [A Portuguese soldier, Diogo Nunes, had entered the same Machiparo province four years before Orellana, and the ten surviving accounts of the 1560 Ursúa expedition independently describe dense settlement, maize stores one chronicler called infinite, long straight roads "in the manner of the Incas of Peru, except for the walls," and ceramics of famous quality]{claim=AMZ-C003} — on the same reaches, from witnesses who were not copying one another and mostly detested each other. None of them repeats Carvajal's giant numbers. All of them repeat his world.

## What the lasers found

{plate:IMG-AMZ-P01}

The modern turn began when researchers noticed geometric ditches emerging from deforested pasture in the Brazilian state of Acre — and accelerated when LiDAR, which strips the canopy off the terrain model, went looking under the trees. In 2026 the survey program led by Martti Pärssinen and Alceu Ranzi published its reckoning: [flying 4,430 kilometers of laser lines, it documented 432 earthworks where satellite imagery had found a fraction of that, and projected roughly 24,000 to 30,000 monuments across a 183,000-square-kilometer cultural region]{claim=AMZ-C004} — as many earthworks in one corner of the basin as had once been guessed for all of Amazonia.

{plate:IMG-AMZ-P02}

Two honesty notes belong here, and this site is built to make them. First: the study proposes a peak population of 1.25 to 3 million people — but [that figure is an assumption engine, and the paper's own alternative reading of the same data yields roughly 250,000 to 600,000]{claim=AMZ-C005}. The monuments are certain; the census is a model. Second, and larger: the Aquiry florescence is dated to roughly AD 100–300. It is decisive evidence that Amazonian environments could carry monument-building societies — and weak evidence about what Orellana saw fourteen centuries later. This case refuses to spend ancient grandeur as contact-era corroboration.

**Transparency.** The 2026 study's funding statement reads, in its own words: "The project and data collection were funded by a grant from E. Jhong (USA)." That is this site's founder. The Athanatos Foundation, with which he is associated, is acknowledged for administrative assistance. Readers should know this — and should notice that it is exactly why this case presents the paper's population claim at its most assumption-exposed, not its most headline-friendly.

What matters for Carvajal is the archaeology whose dates overlap him. [An 1,800-kilometer arc of the southern rim was occupied by earth-building societies in fortified villages around AD 1250–1500]{claim=AMZ-C006}, with its own population model stated as a model. And [several major settlement systems ran into the contact era and beyond: the Belterra Plateau towns near Santarém from about AD 1300 to seventeenth-century colonization, complete with dark earths, ponds, and trodden roads; Marajó's Cacoal site to 1650; and Upper Xingu settlements inhabited until about 1770, their plaza-and-road layouts matching the villages living Kuikuro communities build today]{claim=AMZ-C011}. That last point deserves its own sentence: some of this is not a lost world at all, but a continuous one — collapsed in numbers, unbroken in pattern.

## Engineered ground

{plate:IMG-AMZ-P03}

The subsistence question — *what could they all have eaten?* — was always the strongest scientific objection, and it now has at least one direct answer. [In the Llanos de Mojos, the Casarabe culture ran intensive year-round maize agriculture on a savannah it had replumbed: drainage canals for the wet season, farm ponds for the dry, documented by phytoliths in the fields themselves]{claim=AMZ-C007}. More broadly, [dark earths, raised-field complexes, forest islands, and orchard-like concentrations of domesticated trees around archaeological sites record long human engineering of Amazonian ground]{claim=AMZ-C008}.

{plate:IMG-AMZ-P04}

But engineering is not a census, and this case keeps the distinction structural. [The raised fields of the Bolivian lowlands, dated carefully, show intermittent construction and use — plausibly flood-risk works built where nothing else would do — not a permanent breadbasket]{claim=AMZ-C009}. And the domesticated-forest signal itself carries a first-class qualifier: five post-Columbian centuries have shaped the modern forest too, and the formal critique arguing they did most of the shaping is part of this record, not a footnote to it.

## How many people?

The honest answer to the famous question is that the published numbers — one million, five, six, ten — are not competing measurements. They are competing models, and several descend from the same assumptions. The field's most influential estimator said so himself: [Denevan publicly rejected the habitat-density method behind his own 5.1–6.8 million figure, while still judging "at least 5 to 6 million" reasonable on other grounds]{claim=AMZ-C012}. A number can outlive the death of the argument that produced it; a reader deserves to know when that has happened.

What the aggregate evidence does support: [radiocarbon proxies for the whole biome fit a population growing logistically toward carrying capacity right up to European arrival]{claim=AMZ-C015} — no sign, at basin scale, that Amazonian societies were already dying out on their own before the caravels.

## The disappearance

If Carvajal described a real world, where did it go? The documented answer, where colonial records permit counting, is brutal and specific. [In the Napo–Aguarico province, an estimated 49,000–51,000 people in the early contact period became about 6,000 by 1700 — an 88 percent collapse; neighboring Mainas lost roughly two-thirds — through differing regional mixtures of epidemics, slave raids, missionization, forced labor, revolt, and flight]{claim=AMZ-C010}. Note the mechanism list: people did not only die in place. They were taken, gathered into missions, and they fled. A riverbank empty in 1690 is not evidence it was empty in 1542; it may be evidence of everything that happened in between.

## The sparse Amazon

Everything above could be true and the maximalist picture still false, and the negative evidence is part of this case's spine, not an afterthought. [Across 7,363 recorded archaeological sites, distance from a river is the single strongest predictor of pre-Columbian occupation, with the deep interfluves markedly sparser]{claim=AMZ-C013}. And [where scientists have cored the soils of the western and interfluvial Amazon — fourteen regions and counting — most terra-firme profiles show closed-canopy forest with little to no sign of crops, clearing, dark earths, tree enrichment, or recurrent fire]{claim=AMZ-C014}. The skeptics of the "garden Amazon" are not defending the old impossibility thesis; they are defending proportions. Both sides' sampling is biased — spectacular sites oversampled, tiny soil cores over-extrapolated — and the case logs both biases rather than choosing one.

## Where this case lands

[The pre-contact Amazon that survives every filter in this record is a strongly heterogeneous one: substantial populations, engineered subsistence, and settlement systems along favored corridors and hotspots, embedded in a much more lightly occupied forest matrix]{claim=AMZ-C016}. Neither the pristine wilderness nor the wall-to-wall civilization survives contact with the evidence; what remains open is proportions.

And the friar? [Carvajal now looks substantially credible about the qualitative social geography he described, and untrustworthy about his extreme numbers]{claim=AMZ-C017} — which is a more interesting vindication than the wholesale kind, because it was earned by discounting everything discountable and testing what was left. The decisive test has not yet been run, and it is this case's flagship research item: classify his populated and empty reaches from the manuscripts alone, model the route's uncertainty, and only then check the archaeology against them, corridor by corridor.

## Credit where the record supports it

One more genealogical fact belongs in the open. [Years before the 2026 results, Graham Hancock's *America Before* devoted seven chapters to the Amazonian discoveries — the Acre geoglyphs among them — and his 2024 series put Pärssinen and Ranzi themselves in front of a mass audience]{claim=AMZ-C018}, at a time when this research was marginal to the mainstream conversation. On this site, outsider status is not evidence — in either direction. Hancock's larger lost-civilization thesis is not what this evidence establishes, and the mainstream's numerical skepticism about Carvajal has been partly vindicated by the manuscript work. But a truthful genealogy records who kept the question alive while the instruments were catching up, and this is such a record.
