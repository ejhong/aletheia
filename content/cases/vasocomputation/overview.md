There is a strange gap at the center of musculoskeletal medicine. Anyone with a foam roller and twenty minutes can locate [dozens of small, discrete, exquisitely tender points in their own body]{claim=VASO-C001} — most densely around the hips and shoulders, more numerous with every passing decade. Roll across one and it announces itself unmistakably. Yet open the research literature and you will find scientists arguing about what these points are — and, in part, [about whether the "trigger point" is a real, reliably identifiable thing at all]{claim=VASO-C030}.

This case is about that gap, and about a chain of ideas that may close it — running from classical muscle physiology through a radical hypothesis about blood and thought, all the way to Tibetan contemplative anatomy. The chain starts on solid experimental ground and climbs into open speculation. The rungs are labeled along the way; the argument ladder below this article makes the decay visible.

## A phenomenon without a mechanism

Part of the reason the fight has run so long is structural. Modern medicine is organized by organ system: orthopedics owns bones, neurology owns nerves, cardiology owns the pipes. Nobody owns muscle tone — the continuous, neurally-set background contraction of soft tissue. No specialty means no billing codes, no imaging protocols, no funding lines. The skeptics' strongest argument — "there is no evidence" — is quietly circular: there is little evidence in part because no one has ever been paid to look properly. But the skeptical case deserves its full weight, and it is heavier than proponents usually grant: systematic reviews found that examiners cannot reliably agree on where trigger points are, there is no validated reference standard for what one is, and the most pointed peer-reviewed critique argues the whole construct rests on circular reasoning. The dispute is not only about mechanism. It reaches the observation base itself.

What survives the critique is a set of instrumented findings. Microdialysis at active trigger points found [the local fluid markedly acidic and loaded with pain mediators — substance P, CGRP, bradykinin]{claim=VASO-C005}; ultrasound studies report the sites stiffer than surrounding tissue, with abnormal local blood flow. Something is there, and it is chemically angry. And practitioners report a consistent phenomenology that any candidate mechanism must explain: the obstruction feels like a dull blockage rather than a sharp stretch-pain, as though the tissue layers themselves had stopped sliding; knots accumulate with age and hold flexibility hostage; and — the strange one — [pressure locates a knot, but release comes with breath: press in, exhale slowly, and something lets go in seconds]{claim=VASO-C004}. That last observation is first-person, from the case author's own practice among many others, and this case labels it as such: it is the anchor phenomenon of everything that follows, and no one has ever measured it.

## Two experiments that catch the loop in motion

The evidence that these spots are under direct neural control has existed for decades, largely unread. In 1993, Hubbard and Berkoff inserted fine needle electrodes into trapezius trigger points and recorded [continuous spontaneous electrical activity confined to a millimeter-scale nidus — chatter in muscle supposedly at rest, vanishing a centimeter away in non-tender tissue]{claim=VASO-C002}. The knot is not merely stiffer than its surroundings; it is a small island of maintained activity.

The follow-up, from McNulty, Gevirtz and colleagues in 1994, is the remarkable one. With electrodes in place, they gave subjects mental arithmetic. [Activity at the trigger point rose; activity at a control site in the same muscle did not; the subject was not tensing]{claim=VASO-C003}. A purely mental event, involving no movement, selectively turned up the electrical activity inside one tender point. The brain has the knot's address. Both studies are small, old, and from one lineage — but under every contested interpretation of what the chatter is, the core finding survives: a discrete tender spot maintained by ongoing neural activity, dialed up by stress.

And in August 2026, a study in Nature Human Behaviour supplied the regulatory counterpart. Mizrachi and colleagues induced identical histamine skin-prick inflammation in the same people twice, manipulating only where attention went. [Attending to the inflamed site produced roughly 1.5-fold smaller, faster-resolving responses in about 90% of participants — with no difference in arousal, and heart-rate variability rising under internal attention]{claim=VASO-C006}. Cognition can write tension into a specific peripheral site; attention and its autonomic train can regulate peripheral physiology back down. The loop's existence is no longer speculative. What remains speculative is what, physically, does the holding.

## The ratchet in the vessel walls

{plate:IMG-VASO-P02}

The body contains a second muscular system under no direct voluntary control: smooth muscle, lining the blood vessels, gut, and airways. Smooth muscle has a property skeletal muscle lacks, textbook physiology since Hai and Murphy formalized it in 1988: [the latch-bridge state, in which its molecular motors grip and then lock, holding tension at a small fraction of the energetic cost after the activating signal has faded]{claim=VASO-C010}. Arteries must hold tone for a lifetime; the latch is how they afford it. It is a kinetic trap, not damage — latched tissue looks normal under a microscope. It is remote-controlled by autonomic state: stress chemistry makes latches form more easily, and the relaxation pathways actively pick the locks. And it exhibits hysteresis — the tissue's present state depends on its history. A cheap, history-dependent hold that persists after its input vanishes is, in engineering terms, a memory element. The question is whether anything ever gets written to it.

Here the speed of release becomes the crucial clue. Structural change in tissue — actual remodeling — takes days to weeks. [A change that completes in five seconds, mid-exhale, must be a change of maintained state, not of structure]{claim=VASO-C011}: something the body is actively doing and can stop doing. Scar tissue does not release on an exhale. The inference is sound; its premise — that the felt release corresponds to an objective local event at all — is precisely the thing never yet measured.

## What if blood talks back?

In 2008, the neuroscientists Christopher Moore and Rosa Cao proposed the hemo-neural hypothesis: that the coupling between neurons and blood runs both ways — that [local vascular state actively modulates what nearby neurons compute]{claim=VASO-C012}, through mundane physical channels: mechanical deformation sensed by pressure-gated ion channels, temperature, chemistry. The proposal looked exotic in 2008. It looks less so now: the Piezo mechanosensors earned a Nobel Prize in 2021, and in 2024 a study in Science demonstrated blood-pressure pulsations modulating neuronal activity through mechanosensitive channels in rats. The read path exists. And nothing in the logic is brain-specific — the periphery has the same ingredients, sometimes in stronger form: dorsal root ganglia wrapped in capillaries behind a leaky blood barrier, sensory endings literally wrapped around the vessels whose diameter they would be sensing.

## The synthesis, and what it explains

The researcher Michael Edward Johnson has assembled these pieces into a hypothesis he calls vasocomputation — and here the argument moves onto its speculative upper floors. Neural activity writes to the vasculature (established coupling). The vasculature can store what is written, via the latch (established physiology, speculatively applied). The stored state reads back onto neural function (the hemo-neural path). Write, store, read: [vascular tension as a memory medium participating in mental life — with the grasping that Buddhism calls tanha as its felt signature]{claim=VASO-C021}.

Whatever the fate of that identification, consider what the stack offers the humble muscle knot. [A knot becomes a latched arteriole throttling its own neighborhood — chronic local ischemia at near-zero energetic cost, producing exactly the acidic, sensitized milieu the microdialysis found, wrapped in secondary skeletal-muscle guarding]{claim=VASO-C020}. This would patch the oldest hole in trigger-point theory: the classic "energy crisis" model needed fibers in permanent maximal contraction, a metabolic bonfire nobody could explain, where a latch holds for almost nothing. It would explain why knots feel like autonomous objects, why they survive years, why the Doppler literature keeps finding flow behaving as if something upstream were clenched. And it would explain the two-key protocol: smooth muscle takes no voluntary commands, but it answers to autonomic state, and [a slow exhale is the most direct voluntary lever on that state — the one bodily rhythm that is both willed and automatic]{claim=VASO-C013}. Pressure perturbs the local loop; breath shifts the chemistry that governs unlatching. The roller is the read head; the exhale is the write head.

## The conventional account, taken seriously

Every one of those explananda has a soberer candidate explanation, and honesty requires displaying it at full strength. Tender points may be neuromuscular guarding: sustained low-level motor drive — the EMG chatter is, after all, skeletal-muscle electrophysiology by every published interpretation. The felt release may be expectation and attention doing what expectation and attention demonstrably do, riding on the genuine but global parasympathetic shift of slow breathing. The critical research report commissioned for this case graded the two linchpin propositions — that a knot objectively releases in seconds on an exhale, and that the releasing element is vascular smooth muscle — grade D: no direct evidence located, in either direction. No study has ever identified a smooth-muscle latch at a tender point. Nor is the arteriole simply unexamined: in pressurized skeletal-muscle arterioles — the exact compartment the hypothesis names — the published measurements report sustained constriction accompanied by maintained or rising myosin light-chain phosphorylation, that is, maintained activation rather than the economical, dephosphorylated hold a latch requires, though none of those studies was designed as a latch test. The energetic cost of holding, meanwhile, has never been measured in any arteriole at all; the cheap-hold argument rests on large conduit-artery preparations, and the one published comparison of a vessel with its own downstream branch found latch behaviour in the parent and absent one branch order later. The mechanism is borrowed from vascular physiology, not yet observed in the tissue it is supposed to explain.

## The oldest anatomy text

{plate:IMG-VASO-P01}

Essentially every contemplative tradition that mapped the inner body arrived independently at the same three claims: there are knots, breath is the tool, and untying them changes consciousness. Tibetan tantric physiology describes channel-knots — rtsa mdud — constricting the flow of wind, untied by breath practice; Hindu yoga names the granthis; seventh-century Chinese medicine documented ashi points, tender spots named for the patient's cry. The standard modern move is to file this as metaphor. The alternative reading is that [millennia of practitioners with extraordinarily trained interoception were producing first-person cartography of the vascular-autonomic tension system]{claim=VASO-C022} — channels as vasculature, winds as autonomic state, knots as latches. It is at least suggestive that the traditional maps place the important knots along the torso's midline, where the sympathetic chains and dorsal root ganglia actually live. But suggestive is all it currently is: no one has ever coded the traditional locations under blinded rules and tested the alignment against chance, and until someone does, the convergence remains vulnerable to post-hoc matching. The traditions also preserved a warning this case records without endorsing a mechanism: releasing held material faster than the system can integrate is genuinely destabilizing, and the modern literature on meditation's adverse effects says the same.

## The crux

Strip everything away and the disagreement is narrower than either camp admits. Both sides accept the latch as physiology. Both sides accept that slow breathing shifts autonomic state globally. Both sides accept that people feel releases. The whole dispute funnels into one empirical question: when a knot "releases" on a slow exhale, does anything objective happen locally — a stiffness drop, a perfusion rise, time-locked to the breath, exceeding pressure, expectation, and generalized relaxation? If yes, the state-not-structure argument engages and the conventional account owes an explanation of what just changed. If no, the featured hypothesis loses its anchor phenomenon and the knot returns to the guarding-and-interpretation account, where the skeptics have been waiting.

That experiment — elastography and Doppler over a palpated knot, calibrated pressure, factorial controls, a button press at each felt release — would cost less than a mid-size grant and has never been run. Everything above the bottom rung of this case is waiting on it.

## What would settle it

This case does not end in a verdict, because on the present record no honest verdict is available. It ends in a research agenda: seven project designs — one of them, the evidence map of where the latch has actually been demonstrated, already executed, with its findings folded into the account above — from the keystone release-imaging study through the knot census nobody has ever taken — there is no graph of knot burden versus age, anywhere, for a phenomenon millions of people can locate in thirty seconds — to the decisive animal experiment separating vascular memory from vascular plumbing, and the unglamorous scholarship that would test whether the contemplative maps are empirical documents. The machines exist. The practitioners exist. The experiments are small, cheap, and specified below, with what each would tell us in either direction.

Someone should count. Either way, the knots are there. Press into one, and breathe out slowly.
