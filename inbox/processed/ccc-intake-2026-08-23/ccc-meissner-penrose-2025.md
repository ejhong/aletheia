I found it. The response you're most likely thinking of is Meissner &
Penrose, "The Physics of Conformal Cyclic Cosmology," posted to arXiv in
March 2025 (arXiv:2503.24263) — this is Penrose's most recent substantive
defense of the Hawking-point interpretation. (There were earlier, shorter
replies too: the published MNRAS version of the Hawking-points paper
responded to DeAbreu, Contreras & Scott on the circles via the "sky-twist"
ellipticity argument, and Gurzadyan & Penrose's 2010–11 notes contested the
critics' choice of null ensemble. But the 2025 paper is the real "he still
thinks the data supports him" document.)

What the new paper argues, in brief: it introduces a "gravitational wave
epoch" (GWE) to make the aeon-to-aeon crossover work naturally, derives
(using 2-spinor and twistor techniques) a mass-energy conservation law
across the crossover surface, and claims that the temperature rise within
the observed Hawking spots is in quantitative agreement with the masses of
the largest galactic clusters observed in our own aeon. It also tackles a
known embarrassment head-on: the observed angular diameter of the spots is
about twice what should have been expected, which the paper attributes to a
GWE period just after the crossover, before the standard cosmological
epochs begin.

Here's my honest assessment of it, for what it's worth:

What's genuinely good about it. The loudest criticism of the CCC
observational program — made explicitly by the Bodnia (HawkingNet) team —
is that its predictions were qualitative: some spots, somewhere, at some
strength, so no search could ever be decisive. This paper is a real attempt
to fix that. Deriving a relation between spot temperature and
previous-aeon cluster mass is exactly the kind of quantitative machinery a
future, properly designed test would need. If the CCC camp followed through
and published a full signal model (expected abundance, amplitude
distribution, profile), the debate could finally become falsifiable.

What it doesn't do — and this is the crux. The paper takes the 2020
detection as established (it cites the An–Meissner–Nurowski–Penrose result
as "impressive observational evidence") and builds theory on top of it. But
the detection itself is what's contested, and as far as I can see the paper
doesn't engage the statistical critiques at all: Jow & Scott showed that
after marginalizing over the ring size — which was chosen after looking at
the data — the 99.98% confidence falls to 87%, barely more than 1σ, and the
2024 Bodnia et al. analysis found no statistically significant Hawking
points after accounting for spots of a few anomalous pixels, while noting
this doesn't disprove CCC itself — just that refined predictions or better
data are needed. Explaining the detailed properties of a signal whose
statistical existence hasn't survived independent reanalysis doesn't answer
the reanalysis. And there's a structural worry: the size discrepancy (spots
twice as big as causality allows) gets resolved by adding a new epoch after
the fact. That may be right! But it's the pattern critics keep pointing to
— every anomaly in the anomaly gets absorbed by a post-hoc adjustment, so
the theory never carries risk.

Where Penrose's side retains real points. The co-location of the five
strongest spots in both Planck and WMAP genuinely shows the features are on
the sky, not in either instrument's noise — no critic has specifically
dismantled that. And the sky-twist test (signal collapses for elliptical
rings) has, remarkably, never been independently run. But note what the
co-location argument doesn't establish: the critics agree the features are
in the sky; their claim is that an ordinary ΛCDM sky produces such features
at exactly this abundance, so being "real" isn't the same as being
anomalous. That's why the whole dispute reduces to which null hypothesis
defines "chance" — and on that question, I think the critics currently have
the stronger position, because rejecting ΛCDM-spectrum simulations as the
null quietly presupposes the conclusion.

So my overall read: the 2025 paper is a serious and even valuable
theoretical advance, but it defends the interpretation of the signal rather
than rehabilitating its statistical significance, which is where the case
actually broke. Penrose telling you the data still supports him is
consistent with everything here — his group's own analyses do show the
signal under their own protocol. What fourteen years of this exchange have
never produced is a test whose rules both sides fixed before looking. The
Aletheia page you linked lands on that same conclusion, and I think it's
right: a preregistered adversarial reanalysis — size range, statistic,
artifact screen, and look-elsewhere correction frozen in advance, ideally
including the sky-twist test — would settle the existing claims either way,
and the mass-temperature relation in this new paper is exactly the
ingredient that could make such a test binding.
