The illustrated narrative

01

[§ The pattern people see]

Put the images side by side and it is easy to see why people stop and look. A bird-headed figure, a handled shape, hands resting across a stone body: distant objects can share a surprisingly familiar visual grammar.

A resemblance is a starting observation. Its historical meaning depends on the objects' dates, contexts, differences and possible connections. The investigations on this site explore pieces of that question with different methods. None is a verdict on the whole of ancient history. Read the experiments and their limitations .

02

[§ Bird-men]

The examples below range from a bird-headed figure in the Lascaux shaft scene to Egyptian gods, Assyrian genii, costumed figures in the Americas and the birdman imagery of Rapa Nui. They invite comparison, but “bird-man” is a broad description: a deity with a bird's head, a person wearing feathers and a person transforming into a bird need not be the same idea.

The catalogue experiment finds its broad bird-person class in 55% of the 926 sampled traditions. That helps establish a baseline for that category; it does not tell us how often a particular pose, object and arrangement occur together. The image pilots tried to get closer to composition, while exposing how differently AI judges can see the same material.

03

[§ The handbags]

The comparison joins the three arched forms on Pillar 43, a bucket carried by Assyrian genii, handled chlorite objects from the Halil Rud region and the small object held by the figure on La Venta Monument 19. The recurring outline is worth looking at closely.

Shape and function are separate questions. Assyrian ritual context supports a bucket-and-purifier interpretation; the function of the Göbekli forms remains uncertain. A handle is also a practical solution that can recur independently. A useful investigation would compare handle attachment, proportions, decoration and accompanying figures, while retaining uncertainty about what each object was for. See the excavation team's Pillar 43 discussion and the Halil Rud wear study .

Pillar 43, the top register Göbekli Tepe, c. 9500 BCE. Three arched objects with small animals; function unknown.

The banduddû Nimrud, 883–859 BCE. A bucket, named in ritual texts, with the cone used to sprinkle from it.

Chlorite "handbag" Jiroft, Iran, c. 2500 BCE. Hero between serpents; National Museum of Iran. Worn on the handle as if hung on a cord.

Monument 19 La Venta, c. 900 BCE. The seated figure's pouch, usually read as an incense bag.

[§ Pigs, lizards & other stone animals]

[§ Pigs, boars and a compact stone silhouette]

A boar-like museum sculpture Supplied photograph. The visible form can be compared now; the museum and object number still need verification.

The Lingjiatan comparison The supplied Chinese caption identifies tomb 07M23 and a weight of 88 kg. Those details are caption-derived, not independently verified here.

The low, rounded body, raised back, lowered head and projecting snout make an immediately legible visual rhyme. The mouth treatment and outline differ, and the photographs do not establish equal scale. Both anatomy and carving choices could contribute: the next useful comparison is with other securely identified pig and boar sculptures.

[§ Reptile-like forms—and the animal in its setting]

Crouched bodies, open jaws, emphatic limbs The supplied montage brings several objects together. Its images need individual identifications before they become a comparison dataset.

The setting matters too The arrangement is consistent with the Göbekli Tepe Enclosure C entrance discussed by the excavators; the precise photograph credit remains unverified.

The splayed limbs, low bodies and pronounced jaws are worth following as a family of forms. “Lizard” should remain a proposed identification where the animal is uncertain. The excavation team's discussion of Enclosure C distinguishes boars from snarling predators and considers their arrangement together. That context may be as informative as an isolated silhouette.

05

[§ The navel of the world]

A sacred centre described as a navel is an evocative parallel. It is also a linguistic claim that needs its own source history. The Greek omphalos , the gloss of Cusco in Garcilaso's account, Rapanui Te Pito o te Henua and the modern Turkish name Göbekli Tepe are not interchangeable kinds of evidence.

For each, the questions are: what does the expression mean in its language, when is that meaning first recorded, and was it a name the ancient builders used? The myth experiment's scope note matters here: a catalogue category for a world axis does not directly test a place-name etymology. Shared bodily metaphors are possible; a particular inherited usage would need more specific evidence.

The omphalos Delphi, Greece; Hellenistic or Roman copy. Archaeological Museum of Delphi. Netted stone marking the meeting point of Zeus's eagles.

Te Pito Kura, "navel of light" Rapa Nui. The iron-rich sphere near Ahu Te Pito Kura, with the fallen giant Paro nearby.

"Navel hill" Göbekli Tepe, Enclosure D. The Turkish name describes the mound's shape; the Kurdish names mean "wish hill."

06

[§ Hands on the belly]

The hands and arms are part of what makes these monuments feel related. Compare where the hands meet, the direction and length of the fingers, the belt, and whether the figure holds anything. A hand across the abdomen and a hand at the groin are close in outline but different observations.

Carving limbs against a stone body can protect them from breakage, making material constraints a plausible contributor to the pose. That does not settle its symbolic meaning. The useful comparison includes both the particular gesture and other figures made in the same medium, rather than assuming either common descent or coincidence from posture alone.

T-pillar with fox Göbekli Tepe, c. 9500 BCE. The T is a stylised head; the shaft a body. Arms and hands are carved on the central pillars of Enclosure D.

Urfa Man Şanlıurfa, c. 9000 BCE. An early life-size human statue, hands over the genitals.

Karahan Tepe figure c. 9000 BCE. Sister site of Göbekli Tepe; both hands on the phallus.

Moai Ahu Tongariki, Rapa Nui, c. 1250–1500 CE. Hands meet on the abdomen above the belt; the Polynesian tiki convention.

Ponce monolith Tiwanaku, Bolivia, c. 500–900 CE. Hands to the chest holding a beaker and a snuff tablet.

[§ Serpents, and the feathered serpent]

Serpents appear alongside other figures on the objects below; the feathered serpent of Mesoamerica is a more specific combination than the broad category “snake.” A comparison should preserve that distinction, including feathers, head shape, body arrangement and the relationship to a human figure.

The catalogue analysis reports a broad cosmic-serpent class in 37% of its sampled traditions. Its geographical result describes that selected category and dataset. It cannot, by itself, distinguish independent invention from very old transmission or establish the ancestry of an individual carving.

Feathered Serpent Teotihuacan, Mexico, c. 150–250 CE. Temple of the Feathered Serpent.

Quetzalcoatl and Ehecatl Codex, 16th century. The feathered serpent and the wind god as one deity.

Hero between serpents Jiroft, c. 2500 BCE. The master-of-animals scheme on a chlorite "handbag."

08

[§ The people who brought the arts]

The narrative parallel is just as arresting as the visual one: a figure brings knowledge, changes human life and departs. Oannes, Viracocha, Quetzalcoatl and Bochica recur in these comparisons. But the exact arrival, teaching and departure need to be read in each account, rather than supplied by a composite biography.

The original AI lineups did find a similarity signal under their conditions, with serious limits. The source-level follow-up opens the passages themselves: sea arrival and sea departure, particular crafts, catastrophe sequence and return promises. It keeps earlier accounts and later retellings separate. A colonial recording date is a reason to examine mediation, not proof that every detail was invented by a chronicler.

Ea and a fish-cloaked sage Assyria, 1st millennium BCE. The apkallu type that the texts actually name; Oannes in Berossus.

Gate of the Sun Tiwanaku, Bolivia, c. 500–900 CE. The central staff-bearer, often called Viracocha; the identification is modern.

Bearded men, Moche Peru, c. 100–700 CE. Facial hair on pre-Columbian objects should be distinguished from the source history of a particular civiliser story.

Bochica Modern statue, Cuítiva, Colombia. The Muisca civiliser as reimagined in the 20th century.

09

[§ What survives the water?]

Flood stories raise several different questions: whether people remember local disasters, whether particular stories travelled, and whether traditions preserve much older environmental knowledge. None requires every version to retain the same details or to lose its regional character.
The flood experiment maps details in 263 summaries. Its regional patterns are useful observations. Establishing the age of a memory, or showing that a particular detail was borrowed from Genesis, needs additional source and historical evidence. Its limitations now make that distinction explicit.

10

[§ Several histories could produce a resemblance]

[§ Transmission and inheritance]

People carry images, stories and skills. A connection becomes more informative when there are dated intermediates, a plausible route, or distinctive details that occur together and predict further evidence. Regional change is compatible with a much older shared inheritance.
A lost-civilisation account adds stronger claims about the source, its date and its capabilities. Similarity alone cannot establish those additions.

[§ Convergence and retelling]

Similar materials, animals, bodies and problems can produce similar forms. Stories can also become more alike through translation, borrowing and comparative writing. These possibilities need evidence too; calling something an archetype is not a complete explanation.
The best next question is often small: which detail is shared, how early is it attested, and what else would we expect to find if the connection were real?

11

[§ Keep the wonder. Open the evidence.]

A common source, long-distance contact, independent invention and later retelling are different explanations. More than one can be true in different cases. The work here is to make the relevant evidence easier to see and the uncertainties harder to miss.

[§ Look at the thing itself]

A complete object, an identifiable passage, an honest caption. Keep the original beside its interpretation.

[§ Ask a question that can change]

Include plausible alternatives and the strongest differences. An unexpected match or an unresolved source is worth reporting.

[§ Keep the whole record]

Publish methods, results, qualifications and earlier versions. A useful experiment can also be a limited one.

Corrections and better sources are welcome . AI assists the research; its output is not a substitute for the evidence.
