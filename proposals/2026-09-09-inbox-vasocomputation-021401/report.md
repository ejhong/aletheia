The body contains a second muscular system under no direct voluntary control:
smooth muscle, lining the blood vessels, gut, and airways. Smooth muscle has a
property that skeletal muscle lacks, textbook physiology since Hai and Murphy
formalized it in 1988: the latch-bridge state .
Skeletal muscle is expensive. Every second a bicep holds a weight, molecular motor
are cycling and burning fuel; stop paying and the muscle drops. Smooth muscle solv
a different engineering problem. Arteries must hold partial contraction — tone —
continuously, for a lifetime; doing so with continuously cycling motors would be
metabolically ruinous. So smooth muscle evolved a ratchet. Under the right chemic
conditions, its molecular motors grip and then lock , holding tension at a small fract
of the energetic cost, for minutes, hours, or in some tissues essentially indefinitely.
The activating signal can disappear entirely and the grip remains. The founding
laboratory observation is exactly this dissociation: stimulate an artery and the
chemical activation spikes and then falls back toward baseline, while the force simp
stays.

looks normal under a microscope; it is just holding. It is remote-controlled by the
autonomic nervous system: stress chemistry makes latches form more easily and sti
harder, while the body’s relaxation pathways (the nitric oxide cascade among them)
actively pick the locks. And it exhibits hysteresis — the tissue’s present state depend
on its history, not merely its current inputs. Two identical vessels receiving identica
signals can be in different states depending on what happened to them an hour ago

whether anything ever gets written to it.

In 2008, neuroscientists Christopher Moore and Rosalind Cao proposed what they
called the hemo-neural hypothesis. The background is uncontroversial: neural activ
drives local blood flow — this coupling is what fMRI measures. The universal
assumption has been that the traffic runs one way. Blood is the caterer, delivering fu
where the work happens, with no opinions about the computation.
Moore and Cao proposed that the arrow runs both ways: that local vascular state
actively modulates what nearby neurons compute. The candidate channels are
mundane and physical. Vessels dilating and constricting mechanically deform the
surrounding tissue, and neurons carry pressure-sensitive ion channels — the Piezo
channels, whose discovery earned the 2021 Nobel Prize and made this proposal
considerably less exotic than it sounded in 2008. Blood flow changes local temperat
to which channel kinetics are sensitive, and washes signaling molecules such as nit
oxide through the tissue. Astrocytes physically bridge vessels and synapses, position
to translate the state of one into the behavior of the other. The hypothesis remains a
minority position, but the evidence has drifted toward it rather than away — and it
carries a delicious irony. If it is right, decades of brain imaging have been watching
blood on the assumption that it was a proxy for mind, when the blood may have bee
part of the mind all along.

The ceiling question: can a state call a repair
subroutine?

[p. 26]
Everything argued so far operates at the level of tension: holding, releasing, regulat
But at the far edge of the contemplative world sits a more radical claim, and it deser
treatment in the same spirit as the rest of this essay — neither swallowed nor
dismissed. The claim is that deep meditative states don’t just regulate tissue; they
repair it — up to and including the reversal of diagnoses medicine considers
irreversible.
The anecdotes circulate at the wildest end: the retreat culture around figures like Jo
Dispenza trades in accounts of cancer remissions, recovered spinal injuries, and —

labeled as such. But they are anecdotes of a category medicine itself recognizes:
spontaneous remission is real, documented, and unexplained. The Institute of No
Sciences assembled a bibliography of over three thousand published medical case
reports of remission across diseases; Everson and Cole’s classic review documented
spontaneous regression of cancer in the peer-reviewed literature, with melanoma an
renal cell carcinoma — the two most immune-sensitive cancers — strikingly
overrepresented. And when researchers interview radical-remission survivors about
what they did, the common factors cluster heavily around state change : meditation,
emotional release, stress removal, a recovered sense of safety. Whatever is happenin

Could the mechanism reach that far? The answer has three rungs, each more
speculative than the last.
Rung one is already on the ground: cognition demonstrably triggers real repair
programs. The placebo effect is the most replicated finding in this entire territory,
it is badly misnamed — it is not fake medicine but endogenous medicine, externally
triggered. Placebo analgesia is reversed by naloxone, meaning the brain released rea

release; immune suppression can be conditioned like Pavlov’s bell; in the trial that

the mind can call physiological programs. The dispute is about the ceiling.
Rung two is the mundane mechanism, and it accounts for more than skeptics adm
Chronic sympathetic bracing measurably degrades repair: stressed caregivers’ woun

from the map. On this rung, the modest miracle claims — faster healing, resolved
inflammation, improved markers — need no new biology at all. They are what a bod
