# Working report — CCC-001 "The Aeon Before Ours"

## 1. Findings

**F1. A substantive independent circle reanalysis is missing from the ledger, and it supplies a physical explanation the record lacks.** [Bielewicz, Wandelt & Banday 2013, MNRAS 429:1376 (arXiv:1207.6905)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.6905) — opened in full. *Source states* (Abstract; §6 Conclusions; Fig. 2): using a fast-convolution method they computed ring variances for radii 0–90° (previous studies stopped at ~20°) around the two Gurzadyan–Penrose directions (l,b)=(105°,37°) and (252°,−31°); they find no significant number of extreme-variance rings, agreeing with Hajian, Moss et al., Wehus & Eriksen; for (105°,37°) rings above ~10° radius do show systematically low variance, but this disappears once the ℓ≤7 multipoles in the simulations are replaced by the observed best-fit low multipoles (i.e., it is the known large-angle anomalies — quadrupole/octopole alignment, hemispherical asymmetry). Rotating those low multipoles by 120° (Fig. 11) shrinks the regions of extreme low-variance rings. They add an internal-consistency argument: CCC predicts randomly distributed concentric sets in which only a few rings are low-variance, whereas the sky shows *systematically* low variance at large radii clustered in a few large regions — a pattern they judge "not consistent with predictions of the conformal cyclic cosmology scenario". *My inference:* this is a fourth early independent reanalysis (Jow & Scott 2020 cite it as [22] in their history), and it is the only one that names a concrete *cause* for the strongest low-variance direction — large-scale power anomalies, not few-pixel spots (CCC-E012) or generic ΛCDM structure (CCC-E003). It bears on CCC-C002 (undermines), CCC-C010 (supports; adds a mechanism), CCC-C001 (reproduces), and offers a testable alternative for the clustering of ring centres in CCC-C504: low-ℓ power structure naturally makes low-variance directions cluster in a few large patches. The record's "four independent reanalyses" count is actually at least five.

**F2. The article's statement that "no independent group has ever run the sky-twist test" is too strong; the twist comparison has been examined and challenged.** [DeAbreu, Contreras & Scott 2015 (arXiv:1508.05158)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.05158), §3 (text preceding Fig. 5) and Fig. 3. *Source states:* GP13's sky-twist procedure rotates the sky to make approximate ellipses from circles; the authors "explicitly tested this" and found the annular variance profile as a function of radius depends on the amount of twist applied, so the comparison against a fixed GP13 threshold "is not a fair comparison"; a proper ellipse search would need the expected variance computed over the full ellipse parameter space, which they call much more computationally intensive. *Also stated* (§4 Conclusions): the apparent drop in low-variance rings at large radius "is merely a result of the particular search criterion used" and has no significance; and (§2/§3) the striking WMAP asymmetry in ring-centre distribution is weaker in Planck Commander, ~25% of simulations show a larger dipole asymmetry, and the WMAP–Planck difference is attributed to inhomogeneous WMAP noise. They also quantify the null: about half of observers in separate Hubble patches would see *more* concentric low-variance rings than we do. *My inference:* the ellipse search itself has still not been independently run (the article is right about that), but the article's framing that the twist test is a clean self-comparison "with no simulations to argue over" is contradicted — the distortion changes the null expectation itself. This adds to CCC-E008 and qualifies CCC-C503 (both halves: the twist result and the 15–20° radius cut-off) and CCC-C504 (the 2013 asymmetry). The 2020 "83% of 1134 centres in 3% of sky" figure is a different statistic and remains untested independently.

**F3. Jow & Scott 2020 contain two further tests the evidence record does not mention.** [Jow & Scott 2020 (arXiv:1909.09672)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.09672), §3.1, §3.2, Fig. 4, §4. *Source states:* (a) nearest-neighbour separations among the most significant Hawking-point candidates have mean 0.20 with σ=0.03 across simulations and the data lie within 1σ — no anomalous clustering of Hawking points; (b) stacking Planck 2018 SMICA E-mode polarization on 709/730 temperature-selected candidates (|â|>2, two scale choices) shows no persistent polarization structure; (c) adding Planck half-difference noise maps to simulations did not change the statistic distribution. §4 also gives the critics' history of the shifting prediction (low-variance rings → concentric sets → high-gradient rings). *My inference:* (a) is an independent negative on clustering that belongs next to CCC-C504-type anisotropy arguments (for Hawking points, not ring triples); (b) is a new, previously unrecorded negative channel (polarization). Neither addresses the Planck–WMAP co-location of the five strongest spots, so the article's "no critic has specifically dismantled" the cross-map coincidence stands on my reading of this paper; Bodnia et al. (grep of the HTML for co-location terms) also do not address it.

**F4. The erebon/LIGO thread has been taken literally, computed, and found hopeless — and its observational premise has been rebutted.** Three sources, none in the index:
- [Allen 2020, Phys. Rev. Research 2, 012034(R) (arXiv:1910.08213)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.08213), §I, §III eq. (5), §IV.A. *Source states:* explicitly motivated by CCC and citing Penrose arXiv:1707.04169 as its ref. [2], it computes the stochastic GW background from Planck-mass dark-matter decay: the direct contribution gives Ω_GW(25 Hz) < 2×10⁻¹⁰³ (~95 orders below LIGO limits); the GW-memory contribution, 60 orders larger, still gives Ω_GW(25 Hz) < 6.7×10⁻⁴³, "misses detectability by 35 orders of magnitude". Allen also argues that Penrose's spherically symmetric impulsive-Vaidya decay model yields no O(1/r) strain (leading effect O(1/r²), "not a GW"), and that memory arises only because the two decay products define an axis.
- [Creswell et al. 2017 (arXiv:1706.04191, abstract)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04191): the correlated-noise report Penrose 2017 built on — residual-noise correlations between Hanford and Livingston maximal at the same lag as GW150914.
- [Nielsen, Nitz, Capano & Brown 2019, JCAP (arXiv:1811.04071)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.04071), Abstract and §5: applying the same Pearson statistic, "no statistically significant correlations" remain after subtracting a maximum-likelihood waveform; consistent with other residual studies.
*My inference:* CCC-C505 is currently recorded as a bare proposal. The record should show that (i) the premise (correlated residual noise) did not survive independent reanalysis, and (ii) the only quantitative treatment of erebon-decay GW signals concludes LIGO is irrelevant by tens of orders of magnitude for a stochastic background. This undercuts the article's "no one has ever run the search" and "waits for anyone to take it literally," and it reshapes CCC-R002: a data search is not the decisive step; a derivation showing *what* individual-decay signal (if any) reaches a detector is. Caveat, separately: Allen's model is a scalar decaying to two massless particles; Penrose could contest the modelling, and Allen flags the disagreement himself. This does not touch CCC-C007's dark-matter proposal as such — only its detectability clause.

**F5. The proponents' null-ensemble rationale has a stated theoretical basis the ledger does not record.** [Gurzadyan & Penrose 2010b (arXiv:1012.1486)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.1486), opened in full. *Source states:* critics simulate "using the CMB power spectrum for LCDM, while we simulate a pure Gaussian sky plus the WMAP's noise"; their table gives σ≈2.2–2.5 μK for their Gaussian simulations versus ~5 μK claimed by Moss et al.; the justification is that by their Kolmogorov-stochasticity analysis (their ref. [5], Gurzadyan et al. A&A 525 L7, arXiv:1011.3711) the random component is only ~20% of the CMB signal; and they object that critics' simulations show no concentric families. *My inference:* CCC-C003 is confirmed verbatim. But the ledger presents the null dispute as a bare choice; in fact it rests on a contestable prior result (the "weakly random CMB" claim), which has its own critical literature (a 2012 paper titled "Application of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to CMB data: Is the universe really weakly random?" appeared in search results; not opened). The concentric-families objection here was later answered directly by DeAbreu et al. (CCC-E008) — a closure link worth recording.

## 2. Best account

The observational bottom rung is uncontested and now over-determined: low-variance annuli, concentric sets, and ring-gradient "Hawking point" candidates all exist in WMAP and Planck maps and are reproduced by every critic (CCC-E002, E003, E004, E008, E010, E012, plus Bielewicz et al. 2013). The dispute is entirely about the reference class. The proponents' significance (CCC-E001) is measured against an isotropic Gaussian sky with a variance roughly half that of a ΛCDM-spectrum sky, justified by a Kolmogorov-stochasticity argument that the CMB is only ~20% random. Critics reply that the ΛCDM spectrum is a measured fact about the sky, not an assumption, and that the features arise in simulated skies at the observed abundance — DeAbreu et al. put it as: half of all observers would see more concentric rings than we do.

What is new this pass is that the critical side has *mechanisms*, not just null histograms, and there are two of them: known large-angle anomalies (ℓ≤7) drive the systematically low variance of large rings around the prime GP direction and produce clustering of low-variance centres in a few large regions (Bielewicz et al.), while a handful of few-pixel bright/dark spots manufacture concentric low-variance families at small radii (CCC-E012). These are complementary explanations at different scales and together plausibly account for both the concentric families and their anisotropic centres (CCC-C504) — though the latter link is my inference and untested on the 2020 triple catalogue.

The proponents' strongest surviving arguments are: the sky-twist result (CCC-C503), the extreme clustering of ring-triple centres (CCC-C504), and the Planck–WMAP co-location of the strongest Hawking points. Each has a supported reply now on record: the twist procedure alters the null (DeAbreu et al.); large-scale anomalies and inhomogeneous noise can cluster centres, and the 2013 asymmetry is at the ~25% level (Bielewicz; DeAbreu); and co-location shows only that the features are on the sky, which no critic disputes. None of the replies is a completed independent test of the specific 2013/2020 statistics — that is the real gap, narrower than the article states.

For the Hawking points, marginalizing over ring scale drops 99.98% to 87% (CCC-E010), polarization shows nothing at the candidates, candidates do not cluster anomalously, a validated detector finds none (CCC-E011), and Hawking points of 100–400 μK amplitude and 0.7–1.2° extent are excluded (CCC-E012, Bodnia et al. §on pseudo-synthetic training). On the framework's forward predictions, the erebon–LIGO channel is worse off than the record shows: the correlated-noise premise failed replication and the only computed erebon GW background is undetectable by ≥35 orders of magnitude. A mixed reading remains available: CCC as a cosmology is untested rather than refuted; every *specific* observational operationalization so far has been either dissolved by a fair null or shown to be unobservable.
