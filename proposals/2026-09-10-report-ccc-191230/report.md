# Working report — CCC-001 "The Aeon Before Ours" (bounded pass)

## 1. Findings

**F1. The record's claim that "no independent group has ever run the sky-twist test" is too strong: DeAbreu, Contreras & Scott (2015) examined it and explained why it is not a fair comparison.**
Source: [DeAbreu, Contreras & Scott 2015, arXiv:1508.05158 (PDF), §3 final paragraphs and §4 Conclusions](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.05158).
- *Source states (observed):* GP13 "claim that there is a crucial dependence on circularity," obtained by "a procedure that rotates the sky to produce approximate ellipses from circles"; the authors "explicitly tested this and found that the variance profile does indeed depend on the amount of 'twist' applied," so "this is not a fair comparison, particularly when using the threshold defined by GP13" (they point to their Fig. 3, variance profile vs radius). A proper ellipse search would require computing the expected variance over the full ellipse parameter space, which they judge much more computationally intensive.
- *Also states:* the "apparent drop in low variance rings at large annular radius is merely a result of the particular search criterion used and has no statistical significance" (§4); and on the anisotropy of ring centres (§2), the WMAP asymmetry "is not present to the same degree in the Planck Commander data," "about 25% of simulations produce a distribution of low variance directions with a higher dipole asymmetry than seen in the data," and the WMAP–Planck difference "is most likely due to the inhomogeneous noise."
- *Also states (§4):* in a standard ΛCDM universe "about half of the observers in separate Hubble patches will see a greater abundance of concentric low-variance rings" than we do (Fig. 5, Planck vs 1000 simulations).
- *My inference:* The article and CCC-E008 under-use this source. It bears directly on **CCC-C503** (both halves — ellipticity and the 15–20° cutoff) and on **CCC-C504** (the centre-anisotropy family of claims, though the specific "83% in 3%" figure is from the 2020 paper and postdates it). DeAbreu et al. did not *replicate* the twist test with a corrected null; they showed the test as constructed compares the sky to a differently-correlated field, which invalidates the GP13 threshold. The honest statement is: the twist test has been independently examined and found methodologically unfair, but never independently re-run with a fair ellipse null. CCC-E008 currently links only CCC-C001/C002; it should also link C503 and C504.

**F2. Jow & Scott (2020) contains three results the ledger does not record beyond the 99.98%→87% headline, one of which weakens the "cross-map coincidence" argument the article leaves standing.**
Source: [Jow & Scott 2020, arXiv:1909.09672 (PDF)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.09672).
- *Source states (§2):* adding Planck half-difference maps as a noise estimate "did not alter the estimated probability distributions," so "instrumental noise does not contribute significantly to the HP signal at the scales we are considering."
- *Source states (§3.1):* a nearest-neighbour test on the 30 most significant HPs at each scale gives ⟨θ_NN⟩ = 0.20 and 0.19 rad against a simulated Gaussian expectation of 0.20 ± 0.03 rad — "we do not find any evidence that HPs exhibit any anomalous clustering on the sky."
- *Source states (§3.2, Fig. 4):* stacking Planck 2018 SMICA E-mode maps on 709 and 730 temperature HPs (|â|>2) shows no persistent polarization features.
- *Source states (§4):* Gaussian simulations contain a more significant HP signal than the data "13% of the time (more if we marginalized over a wider range of scales)"; the only way the conclusion could have been avoided is "if the predictions of CCC could have been made much more precise before looking at the data." §4 also gives a compact history of the shifting CCC prediction: low-variance rings → concentric sets → high-gradient rings.
- *My inference:* The article says the WMAP/Planck co-location of the strongest spots is something "no critic has specifically dismantled." That is literally true, but Jow & Scott's noise result makes it moot: if noise is negligible at HP scales, every strong feature of the *CMB signal itself* must appear in both maps, so co-location confirms only that the spots are sky, which no critic disputed. The polarization null is a genuinely new negative line (recordable under negative-evidence policy), with the caveat Jow & Scott themselves give — CCC makes no polarization prediction, so this is a weak-diagnosticity null. The clustering null is the HP analogue of CCC-C504 and should be linked to CCC-E010.

**F3. A missing independent reanalysis — Bielewicz, Wandelt & Banday (2013) — offers a *mixed* explanation of the circle statistics the ledger currently frames as a binary.**
Source: [Bielewicz, Wandelt & Banday 2013, MNRAS 429, 1376, arXiv:1207.6905 (PDF), Abstract and Conclusions](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.6905). (Cited by Jow & Scott as their ref. [22]; not in `index`.)
- *Source states:* using a fast-convolution variance method over radii 0–90° on WMAP7, for one of the two GP special points, (l,b)=(105°,37°), rings with radii >10° have "systematically lower variance" than ΛCDM simulations; "after replacing the ℓ∈[0,7] multipoles in the simulations with the best-fitting values from the foreground corrected V-band data ... the agreement between simulations and the observed variances is very good." Low-variance rings "can be easily found centred on other points in the sky." A χ² statistic for large radii "takes smaller values than simulations ... at a significance level around 95%," and the north/south disparity in low- and high-variance rings weakens after removing or rotating the ℓ≤7 multipoles. They conclude CCC "predicts randomly distributed sets of concentric rings," whereas the data show rings "clustered in a few relatively large regions of the sky," so the results "are not consistent with" CCC.
- *My inference:* This is not another "nothing to see" reanalysis. It says the sky *is* mildly non-standard on large scales, that this is the already-known low-ℓ anomaly, and that this same anomaly produces both the large-radius low-variance rings and their clustering into a few regions. That gives a conventional, non-CCC account of the centre-clustering the proponents read as previous-aeon superclusters (CCC-C504), and it refines CCC-C003: the dispute is not only Gaussian-vs-ΛCDM null, but also whether the null should condition on the observed low-order multipoles. It bears on CCC-C002, C003, C504 and C010, and its Fig. 8–11 p-value maps are candidate plates.

**F4. The erebon/LIGO thread (CCC-C505, CCC-R002) has a substantive literature the ledger does not hold, and it runs against the article's "no one has ever run the search" framing.**
- [Creswell et al. 2017, arXiv:1706.04191 (abstract)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04191) — *states:* correlations in detector noise around GW150914 "happen to be maximized for the same time lag as that found for the event itself." This is the premise Penrose adopted.
- [Penrose 2017, arXiv:1707.04169 (PDF, Abstract and §1–2)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.04169) — *states:* the correlated "noise" may be erebon decays in the BBH's host galaxy (dark-matter density "around a hundred times" intergalactic); the geometry is an "impulsive Vaidya metric" (Schwarzschild converting to Minkowski along an outgoing spherical null shell); and proposes a concrete test — look for correlated noise at the inter-detector delay corresponding to a prominent galaxy "say the Andromeda galaxy." *Inference:* the ledger's C505 could record that Penrose specified a testable protocol, not merely a conjecture.
- [Nielsen, Nitz, Capano & Brown 2019, arXiv:1811.04071 (abstract)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04071) — *states:* using the Liu–Jackson/Creswell statistic on LIGO's released data, "after subtracting the maximum likelihood waveform there are no statistically significant correlations between the residuals of the two detectors." *Inference:* the empirical premise of C505 was contested and, on this analysis, removed.
- [Allen 2020, Phys. Rev. Research 2, 012034, arXiv:1910.08213 (PDF)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.08213) — *states:* the paper "was originally motivated by Penrose's 'conformal cyclic cosmology'"; the "direct" graviton spectrum from Planck-mass erebon decay gives Ω_GW(25 Hz) < 2×10⁻¹⁰³, "below detectability in LIGO by about 95 orders of magnitude" (§III, Eq. 5); the "memory-effect" background gives Ω_GW(25 Hz) < 6.7×10⁻⁴³, missing "detectability by 35 orders of magnitude" (§IV, Eq. 10); and on Penrose's spherically symmetric impulsive-Vaidya picture, "for an isotropic source there is no O(1/r) GW term in the strain," the leading effect being O(1/r²) and "negligible for distant sources" (§IV.A). Conclusion: "current technology offers no prospects for constraining or detecting such backgrounds."
- [Ulhoa et al. 2026, arXiv:2607.10816 (abstract page only)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10816) — *states:* extending Creswell's cross-correlation to nine O2/O3 events and three detector pairs, in 26 of 27 pair-measurements the event peak is indistinguishable from the off-source noise ensemble; the authors read persistent correlation structure as possibly a stochastic GW background. They do not mention erebons in the abstract. Not opened in full; low weight.
- *My inference:* Allen's is a narrow-model result — a homogeneous cosmological decay population and an idealized two-body decay — and must not silently become a verdict on the whole erebon hypothesis (CCC-C007). But it does directly address the mechanism Penrose invoked, and it converts CCC-R002 from "nobody has looked" into "the one quantitative calculation says the signal is unobservable by tens of orders of magnitude, and the original noise correlation is disputed." CCC-E019 (strength: weak, direction: context) is roughly right, but the ledger lacks the reply chain.

## 2. Best account

The observational floor is uncontested and now has more than four independent confirmations: low-variance annuli, concentric sets, and high-gradient rings are all genuine outputs of the published procedures on WMAP and Planck (CCC-C001). Everything contested lives one rung up. The strongest critical account is layered rather than single: (i) the abundance of concentric low-variance sets is typical of ΛCDM Gaussian skies, with about half of hypothetical observers seeing more than we do (DeAbreu et al. §4); (ii) the large-radius deficit and the regional clustering of ring centres are the known low-ℓ anomaly wearing a different statistic (Bielewicz et al.) — a *mixed* explanation in which the sky is mildly unusual on large scales for reasons that predate and do not require CCC; (iii) the Hawking-point 99.98% is a look-elsewhere artefact (87% after marginalization), the HPs do not cluster anomalously, and they leave no E-mode counterpart (Jow & Scott §§2–4); (iv) at full resolution, few-pixel artefacts manufacture the ring criterion (CCC-E012, not re-opened this pass).

The proponents' surviving arguments should be stated fairly. The sky-twist test is the best of them because it needs no simulation — but it has now been shown that twisting the sky changes the variance profile against which "low" is judged, so the test as run compares apples with pears (DeAbreu et al. §3). It has *not* been re-run with a fair ellipse null; that remains a real, narrower gap. The WMAP–Planck co-location of the strongest HPs establishes the spots are on the sky, but since instrumental noise is negligible at HP scales (Jow & Scott §2), co-location is expected under every hypothesis including ΛCDM; it is nearly non-diagnostic. The 2020 anisotropy figure (83% of centres in 3% of sky) has not been specifically re-tested, but two independent results — DeAbreu's dipole-asymmetry test and Bielewicz's low-ℓ diagnosis — give a conventional mechanism for exactly that kind of clustering.

On the forward-prediction side (erebons), the situation is worse for CCC than the article suggests. The LIGO noise correlation Penrose seized on was not confirmed on reanalysis with a maximum-likelihood subtraction (Nielsen et al.), and the only published calculation of the GW signature of Planck-mass decays finds it 35–95 orders of magnitude below LIGO's reach and finds no far-field GW strain at all from the spherically symmetric geometry Penrose described (Allen). This is not a refutation of erebons as dark matter — Allen's model is idealized — but it removes the "lurking in LIGO noise" route unless a different emission mechanism is specified.

The methodological verdict in the adopted assessment stands: "contradicted" for the detection claims, "untested" for CCC as a cosmology, and no preregistered analysis on either side. What this pass adds is that the critical case is more textured than "the null was wrong" — it includes a positive alternative for the clustering (low-ℓ anomalies) and a positive reason the co-location argument cannot discriminate.
