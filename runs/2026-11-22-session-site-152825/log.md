## What I found

Each name was read from the oldest text I could reach:

- **Delphi.** The usage is Greek and the sanctuary's own. Pindar's Pythian 4 (462 BCE) has an oracle "spoken beside the central navel of well-wooded mother earth". Strabo (9.3.6) says people called Delphi the navel of the earth, and Pausanias (10.16.3) that the Delphians said their white-marble Omphalus was the centre of all the earth. Strabo also gives the story of Zeus's two eagles meeting there. He reports it as a myth told by Pindar, calls it a fabrication, and notes a variant with crows. He describes a navel-stone in the temple "draped with fillets" with likenesses of the two birds on it.
- **Cusco.** The city name is the Incas' own; the "navel" is a gloss. The earliest record found is Garcilaso's *Comentarios Reales* (Lisbon, 1609), read in Markham's 1869 translation. Garcilaso says Cuzco means a navel "in the special language of the Yncas" (Book I ch. XVIII, p. 73). In Book II ch. XI (p. 142) he calls it "the navel of the earth", fitting a city at the centre of a country "long and narrow, like a human body".
- **Rapa Nui.** The early written record already disagreed, and this is the finding I did not expect. Thomson (1891) glosses Te Pito te Henua as "navel and uterus" and reports that, by tradition, Hotu Matua gave the name. Churchill (1912) reports that Thomson and Roussel agree on the name and disagree on its meaning. He rejects Thomson's "story of the antiquity of this name" and reads pito as "end", so that the phrase means "the end of the land". He also reports that an islander shown a chart said "there are three" and pointed to the island's headlands. Churchill concludes that the phrase was not the island's name in indigenous usage. This became the new claim DEEP-C093.
- **Göbekli Tepe.** The site's UNESCO presentation on Google Arts & Culture renders the name as "potbelly hill", a name describing the mound's appearance: a description, not a sacred name, and not quite "navel hill". Schmidt 2010 dates the mound to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. He gives no history of the name, but he notes a wishing tree on the ridge that local people still visit. That is entered only as context for the Kurdish "wish hill" claim, DEEP-C016, which stays unsourced.
